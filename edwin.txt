1) Introduction  
1.1) 
Coupled physical pendulums  
Simple harmonic motion refers to the motion of a body when the restoring force (on the 
body) is directly proportional to the displacement from equilibrium1. Such conditions are met 
in a physical pendulum, which is a rigid body that undergoes fixed axis rotation about a fixed 
point2.  
A two-mass coupled pendulum is formed when two identical pendulums are attached by a 
soft spring3. The spring in between is assumed to obey Hooke’s law (i.e., an ideal spring), 
which states that the displacement or size of the deformation (of the spring) is directly 
proportional to the deforming force or load4.  
1.2) 
Modes of oscillation  
A single oscillator has a single natural frequency. However, when two or more oscillators 
are coupled with springs, they have several natural frequencies and that the general motion 
(of the oscillator) is a combination of variations at all different natural frequencies5  
In the case of a oscillations involving two coupled oscillators, the mode of oscillation is 
divided into two normal modes of oscillation, which are modes at which the masses can 
oscillate sinusoidally, and the modes which the masses oscillate depend on the initial 
conditions of oscillation. The pendula will oscillate in the 1st normal mode of oscillation 𝜔1, 
in which the pendula solely oscillates in the 1st normal angular frequency, when the masses 
are initially displaced in phase. While the pendula will oscillate in the 2nd normal mode of

1.3) 
Research question  
Coupled pendulums not only frequently appear in clock machineries, and the 
mechanism of coupled pendulums are also present in molecular vibrations, in which bonds 
within a molecule are distorted in the same way as springs are stretched and compressed.  
 
The bond strength between different diatomic molecules vary, which in turn affects 
the atoms’ frequency of vibration. I am interested in investigating such a situation but 
examining molecular vibration is out of my reach. Instead, such a situation can be simulated 
classically, by varying the coupling strength between two masses in a coupled pendulum, 
which can be done so by changing the position of the spring from the pivot.  
In addition, upon the various possibilities of sending the coupled pendulum in oscillation, I 
would like to particularly investigate on the oscillation of pendulums in resonance as it 
exhibits the superposition of the 2 normal modes of oscillation, allowing me to obtain both 
𝜔1 and 𝜔2, and thus, 𝜔𝑎𝑣𝑔 and 𝜔𝑏𝑒𝑎𝑡 in one go, rather than conducting the experiment in 
both in-phase and out-of-phase measurement. Therefore, I would like to investigate “In a 
coupled physical pendulum with pendula oscillating in resonance, how does distance of 
spring from pivot affect the 1st and 2nd normal mode angular frequencies, hence, the 
average and beat angular frequencies?”  
2) 
Qualitative prediction  
As the distance of the spring from the pivot increases, the torque exerting on the arms of the 
pendula increases. The greater the torque acting on the pendula, the greater the 𝜔2. However, 
the 𝜔1 will remain unchanged since it is independent of the spring extension. As 𝜔𝑎𝑣𝑔 and 
𝜔𝑏𝑒𝑎𝑡 is a linear combination of the 2 normal mode frequencies, an increases distance of the 
spring from the pivot would result in an increase in the magnitude of both 𝜔𝑎𝑣𝑔 and 𝜔𝑏𝑒𝑎𝑡.

Experimental procedures 
7.1) 
Determination of spring constant  
1. Attach the spring on a stand and clamp and measure its equilibrium length.  
2. Hang 20g slotted masses on the lower end of the spring and record the final length of 
the spring.  
3. Deduct the equilibrium length of the spring from its final length to obtain the spring 
extension 𝑥.  
4. Repeat step 2 and 3 for 5 more times (with a total of 6 slotted masses).  
5. Obtain the weight 𝑊 of the slotted masses using 𝑊= 𝑚𝑔, where 𝑚 is the total mass 
of the slotted masses in kg.  
6. Plot a graph of weight  𝑊 against extension ∆𝑥, apply a linear line of best fit and plot 
the worst lines.  
7. The slope of the line of best fit is the spring constant of the spring, and the uncertainty 
of the slope can be found by ∆𝑚=
𝑀𝐴𝑋(𝑚)−𝑀𝐼𝑁(𝑚)
2
, where 𝑀𝐴𝑋(𝑚) is the slope of 
the maximum worst line and 𝑀𝐼𝑁(𝑚) is the slope of the minimum worst line 
7.2) 
Finding the first and second normal mode frequencies 
1. Create an FFT spectrum and an angle-time graph of one of the pendulums on the 
PASCO Capstone data-logging interface.  
2. Set the horizontal axis of the FFT spectrum to a maximum of 2.0Hz.  
3. Set the maximum number of bins to 4096 in FFT properties.  
4. Set the sample rate of the rotary motion sensor to 40Hz.  
5. Keep one of the pendulums at rest and displace the other to an arbitrary small angle 
from equilibrium.  
6. Press “Start recording”. After 20 seconds, press “stop recording.”  
7. Use the coordinate tools on the FFT spectrum, find the 2 highest peaks on the graph to 
obtain the frequencies of the first and second normal mode of oscillation.

12) Evaluation  
The systematic errors in this experiment mainly stem from the assumptions made 
when deriving the theoretical model to explain the relationship between the angular 
frequencies of oscillation and the length of the spring from the pivot. The assumptions made 
in the model were small angle approximation, assumption of the same masses of the pendula, 
and the absence of any external damping forces. Below are the evaluation of such factors and 
the significance on how they would affect the experimental results systematically, in addition 
with the random errors, and how such errors can be reduced to give more accurate and 
precise results respectively.
