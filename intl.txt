Introduction
1.1
RC Circuits and Low-Pass Filters
A resistor-capacitor circuit, abbreviated as a RC circuit, is a circuit which contains a resistor and a capacitor
connected in series.
A low-pass filter is a filter that passes signals with a frequency lower than a selected cutoff frequency, fc,
and attenuates signals with frequencies higher than fc (see [1]). An ideal low-pass filter would allow all signals
with frequency lower than fc to completely pass through, and block all signals with frequency higher than fc.
The transfer function of a system measures the magnitude response as a function of input frequency (see
[2]). The magnitude response is defined as the ratio between the output magnitude and the input magnitude.
For example, an ideal low-pass filter has magnitude response 1 for all frequencies below fc; and magnitude
response 0 for all frequencies above fc. The magnitude response can be plotted against the frequency to give
Figure 1.
Figure 1: Magnitude transfer function for an ideal low-pass filter
RC circuits can act as low-pass or high-pass filters. If the voltage output is taken over the capacitor, then
it will act as a low-pass filter; and if taken over the resistor, it will act as a high-pass filter (see Figures 2, 3).

The power spectral density (PSD) is a function that describes how the power of the signal is distributed
across various frequencies. In accordance with the physics and engineering convention, I will use the one-sided
PSD, which means that only positive frequencies are included.

in reality, as that would imply that the signal has infinite power. So an upper limit on the frequency, i.e. a
bandwidth, must be set. Let us call this bandwidth B (with units Hz).

1.4
Motivation and Research Question
In general, noise reduction has many practical applications. For example, in daily life, noise reduction is
applied in phone calls to improve call clarity and reduce background interference. Other applications include
medical diagnostic tools like electrocardiograms and electroencephalograms, as well as in audio and image
processing.
In this essay, I aim to explore the noise reduction effect of RC low-pass filters. More precisely, I would like
to answer the question: How does the frequency of an input sine wave f ∗, with background white noise, affect
the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) after noise reduction through a RC low-pass filter?
2
Qualitative Prediction
If the input sinusoidal signal has a high frequency, it will be removed by the low-pass filter, leading to
weak signal power and thus low SNR. Conversely, if the input sinusoidal signal has a low frequency, it will
pass through the low-pass filter, leading to strong signal power; and since high-frequency white noise will be
removed, noise power will be reduced, leading to a high SNR.
3
Hypothesis
As the frequency f ∗of the input sine wave increases, the SNR decreases.

In the real world, we usually deal with sampled signals consisting of discrete data points, rather than

The Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) is precisely that. Mathematically, it is given by

The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is an algorithm to compute the DFT highly eﬀiciently. It is available on
many oscilloscopes for analyzing input signals.
6.1.2
Spectral Leakage, Windowing, and Correction Factors
Suppose a pure sine wave is input into the FFT. Ideally, all its amplitude should be contained in a single
frequency bin. However, in practice, if a sine wave is not right in the middle of a frequency bin, a phenomenon
known as spectral leakage occurs, where the amplitude of the tone is distributed across neighbouring frequency
bins, instead of in a single frequency bin. Spectral leakage in the FFT cannot be removed entirely, but there
are ways to mitigate its effects.
To address the problem, the frame of input data can be multiplied by a window function before performing
the FFT. The purpose is to taper the signal to zero at the edges of the frame, which eliminates the sharp
discontinuity that causes leakage. However, there is always a trade-off between the frequency resolution and the
dynamic range.
The frequency resolution refers to the ability to distinguish two components with frequencies very close to
each other (see [6]). This can be measured with the mainlobe width, which is the width of the central peak in
the FFT frequency domain graph. A wider mainlobe smears the amplitude of a component over more adjacent
frequency bins, causing a lower frequency resolution.
The dynamic range refers to the span of small-to-large frequencies that can be properly measured without
large distortion by the FFT (see [6]). This can be measured by the sidelobe level, which is the height of the side
peaks in the FFT frequency domain graph. Higher sidelobes mean the leakage from a strong signal can be seen
12

--- PAGE 13 ---
far away in the spectrum, making it hard to see a weak signal next to a strong one, causing a lower dynamic
range.
Various windows for the FFT exist, with all of them having a tradeoff between narrowing the mainlobe and
suppressing the sidelobes.

6.2.3
Pre-FFT Data Processing
To find the SNRs experimentally, I need to measure Voutput, signal and Voutput, noise. The latter is a constant,
and its measurement will be addressed in Section 8.1. As for the former, I need to measure the r.m.s. amplitude
of a particular frequency component in a noisy signal, hence the need for the FFT.
The FFT amplitude reading at the frequency component f ∗does not actually represent Voutput, signal. This
is because the noise has some amplitude at f ∗, so the FFT amplitude reading measures the noise and signal
amplitudes at f ∗.
To account for this, I used the data acquisition mode of the oscilloscope to be ”Average: 256 times”. This
means that the oscilloscope averages the readings in the time domain over 256 trials. This was intended to allow
the noise signals to cancel each other out in the time domain, leaving the pure sine wave component, which
could then be used in the FFT.

10
Evaluation of Data
10.1
Systematic Errors
10.1.1
Underestimations
For f ∗= 300, 400, . . . , 1000 Hz, the experimental SNRs are often less than the theoretical SNRs.
It is proposed that this is a procedural systematic error, arising due to the limitations of the apparatus.
When the oscilloscope uses the FFT to compute the amplitude of the SNRs, a phenomenon known as spectral
leakage occurs. Spectral leakage refers to the spreading of a signal’s energy across multiple frequency bins in
a FFT analysis, making a pure tone appear to ”leak” into other frequencies (see [11]). In other words, my
amplitude measurements of the sine wave signal are lower than the true value, causing an underestimation.
In practice, spectral leakage is unavoidable, as this is an inherent limitation in FFT. However, the effects
of spectral leakage can be mitigated. In my experiment, I chose the default Hanning window to carry out the
FFT, which balances frequency resolution and leakage reduction, and is recommended for most signals like sine
29

--- PAGE 30 ---
waves and narrowband random signals (see [12]). However, it is possible to choose other windows which may
have a better effect, as in general different windows work best for different types of signals.
10.1.2
Overestimations
For f ∗= 100, 200 Hz, the experimental SNRs are significantly higher than the theoretical SNRs.
It is proposed that this is a methodological systematic error, arising due to the design of data collection.
Since the amplitude measurement in the FFT takes into account both the sine wave and noise components, the
amplitude I recorded as the amplitude of the signal is actually the amplitude of the signal plus the amplitude of
the noise at f ∗= 100, 200 Hz. The reason that the error is so significant here, but not for higher values of f ∗,
is because the RC low-pass filter greatly reduces the amplitudes of high-frequency noise, so the baseline noise
is negligible at high frequencies. However, f ∗= 100, 200 Hz are low frequencies significantly below the cutoff
frequency of the RC circuit, which is given (see (23) for derivation) by
fC =
1
2πRC = 476.5 Hz.
(47)
This means that my measurements overestimate the amplitude of the signal at low frequencies. Thus, it
seems that the average data acquisition method introduced in Section 6.2.3 is not effective, and not all of the
noise is cancelled out. To rectify this error, I will instead use the ”Sample” data acquisition method for the FFT.
I will take a baseline noise amplitude measurement for each f ∗, then subtract the baseline from the amplitude
measurement used.
10.2
Random Errors
As discussed in Section 4, the instantaneous power of white noise input is random, and cannot be calculated
or predicted.
Therefore, all measurements related to it will vary unpredictably over time.
I have tried to
mitigate this effect by averaging over 5 trials, but it is impossible to eliminate this error.

Based on my suggestions in Section 10, I have developed the following modified methodology:
1. Set the data acquisition mode of the oscilloscope to ”Sample”.
2. Set the FFT window to Rectangle.
3. Set the output frequency of the sine wave signal generator to 100 Hz.

5. Record the amplitude reading.

Conclusion
I addressed the research question: How does the frequency of an input sine wave f ∗, with background white
noise, affect the SNR after noise reduction through a RC low-pass filter?
In the theoretical model in Section 5, I found SNR in terms of f ∗as
SNR =
2πRCV 2
0
N0 arctan (2πBRC)

1 + (2πf ∗RC)2.
(50)
I experimentally verified this relationship in Sections 6 to 10. Moreover, to address the errors discussed
in Section 10, I refined my methodology in Section 10, which reduced overestimating systematic errors, and
investigated the effect of FFT windows on the experimental SNR in Sections 11 to 13, finding that the Blackman
and Rectangle windows perform the best for this experiment. I also simulated the experiment numerically in
Section 14, and confirmed spectral leakage errors as a key contributing factor to underestimating systematic
errors in the experimental sections.
To further improve my experiment, I may address random errors by carrying out more trials per data point,
so I may use standard deviation (as opposed to half-range) for uncertainty analysis.
This investigation focused on low-pass RC filters only. A natural extension is to investigate other filters, e.g.
Sallen-Key filters, which exhibit resonance behaviour (see [16]). Another possible extension is to consider other
types of noise, e.g. pink noise, where PSD is inversely proportional to frequency (see [17]).
